## Test 6 — language supply, consumed language and cross-origin movement — 2026-10

**Question:** which parts of the Test 6 argument are hard to carry in prose — the structure of multilingual supply, the distance between consumed language and reachable language, the shape of the anchor distribution, the comparison between behavioural and opportunity movement, and the size of the programme effect that remained after matching?

**Established:** multilingual supply is heavily structured by programme type; consumed-language concentration sits far above opportunity concentration in every access regime; Baseline anchor strength is continuous apart from a pile-up at single-origin viewing; cross-origin viewing moved several times further than the opportunity behind it, and opportunity direction barely predicts behavioural direction; programme type shifts cross-origin behaviour only modestly once opportunity and activity are comparable.

**Not established:** causal language or programme effects, language-openness psychology, a calibrated selectivity measure, segment membership, persistence or headroom. Baseline against Final remains descriptive, not within-profile longitudinal proof.

### How to read each figure

**Figure 15 — multilingual supply by programme type.** Each horizontal bar is one programme type and totals 100% of that type's parent titles; the x-axis is the share of titles, not of viewing. Grey is monolingual supply, and progressively darker blues are titles offering 2, 3, 4, 5 and 6 audio languages. The whole coloured portion is the programme's multilingual share, printed at the right with the title count, while shade depth describes how deep the audio-track supply goes. Numbers inside segments are parent-title counts. Compare the length of the grey block across programmes — movies against catch-up is the real structural separation. Documentary specials are faded because n = 9. This is supply only: no viewer behaviour is used, and available audio is opportunity rather than consumed audio.

**Figure 16 — consumed against opportunity language HHI.** The x-axis is HHI, where higher means more concentrated; each row is an access regime, with all eligible profiles on top in bold. Within a row, compare the hollow square (concentration of reachable language opportunity, Final) with the filled blue circle (concentration of consumed language, Final); the horizontal distance between them is the gap, printed in the right-hand column beside the share of profiles where consumed exceeds opportunity and the Final profile count. Small grey dots are the Baseline values, shown as context only. Read the separation, not the position of a single marker. Do not read the gap as a calibrated language-selectivity effect: consumption volume is finite and no same-volume random-choice null has been estimated.

**Figure 17A — Baseline anchor strength distribution.** The x-axis is Baseline anchor share in contiguous 5-point bands and the y-axis is the percentage of the 8,199 paired profiles in each band; bars touch because the bands are intervals, so read the shape as a distribution rather than as ranked categories. The body between roughly 30% and 95% declines smoothly with no elbow, which is the evidence that ≥70% is a diagnostic slice rather than a discovered segment. The top band is the exception, and the annotation says what it contains: near single-origin viewing by the lightest viewers with the least cross-origin reach. The exact count at exactly 100% is not published.

**Figure 17B — cross-origin change by anchor band.** Both panels share the same x-axis of Baseline anchor-share bands. The upper panel plots levels — grey for Baseline, blue for Final — against a dashed line showing 1 − anchor share; Baseline tracks that line because the bands are defined from it, so the Baseline slope is definitional rather than behavioural. The lower panel carries the informative comparison: Final minus Baseline in percentage points, labelled per bar, with profile counts printed underneath as support. Compare the change across bands, not the Baseline slope. Bands below 30 profiles are omitted, and the footer states the regression-to-the-mean caution: bands defined on Baseline values will drift toward the middle in Final without any behaviour change.

**Figure 18 — behaviour against opportunity change.** The horizontal axis is the average change in cross-origin opportunity share (title-days) and the vertical axis the average change in cross-origin viewing share (minutes), both in percentage points on one shared scale; bubble area is the number of profiles and the black cross is all 8,199 paired profiles. The dashed diagonal is a 1:1 mechanical response — viewing moving exactly as much as opportunity — so the distance from that diagonal is the point of the figure: opportunity shares moved a few points while viewing shares moved 14 to 25. Groups are defined by the sign of their viewing change, so their vertical position is signed by construction; read distance from the diagonal and bubble size, not quadrant membership. A sign is not a materiality threshold, and nothing here is causal.

**Figure 18B — direction and magnitude companion.** Panel A asks whether opportunity direction predicts behaviour direction: each bar is 100% of the profiles whose opportunity share rose or fell, split by what their viewing did, with the group n at the right. The two bars being near-identical (64% against 66%) is the finding. Panel B puts magnitudes on one percentage-point scale: for each group the hollow square is the mean change in opportunity share and the filled circle the mean change in viewing share, so the length of the connector is the discrepancy. Group means in panel B are signed by construction — compare magnitudes, not signs.

**Figure 19 — programme deviation from matched peers.** The x-axis is the Final cross-origin minute share on its real scale, so the reader can see that every deviation sits on a 40–50% base. For each programme type, compare the hollow square (matched-peer expectation) with the filled circle (observed share); the gap between them is the peer-adjusted deviation, repeated numerically in the right-hand table under both activity controls, beside the all-profiles column, the share above peers and n. Plotted positions use the active-days control on the supported population (peer cell n ≥ 20 — a support rule, not a behavioural threshold). Read Movie, Web series and TV catch-up as the supported results; Reality is faded because its deviation changes sign once thin peer cells are included, and Documentary special is omitted with one supported profile. The deviation is a descriptive association, never a causal effect of programme type.

**Reasoning:** Figure 15 uses part-to-whole because the analytical claim is compositional — how much of each programme type carries multi-track supply — and shade depth adds the second dimension without a second chart. Figure 16 is a dumbbell because the quantity of interest is the *distance* between two measures computed on the same profiles; two separate bar charts would have made the reader subtract by eye. Figure 17 is deliberately split: 17A answers "is there a natural cutoff?" and 17B answers "where did movement actually happen?", and merging them would have hidden the definitional Baseline slope inside a distribution. Figure 18 uses one shared scale with a 1:1 diagonal because the entire argument is that behaviour moved further than opportunity; unequal axes would have destroyed that comparison. 18B exists because the quadrant chart answers the magnitude question well and the direction question poorly, and the direction result — opportunity direction barely predicting behaviour direction — deserved its own readable panel. Figure 19 plots on the outcome scale rather than on a deviation axis so that a ±2-point deviation is visibly small against a 40–50% base.

**Rejected:** a single chart merging anchor distribution and cross-origin movement, because one is a distribution and the other a change across bands. A deviation-only bar chart for Figure 19, because it would have made modest differences look dramatic by hiding the base. Dropping the thin groups from Figures 15 and 19 to tidy the picture — they are faded and labelled instead. Any uncertainty band on these figures: the published evidence carries group means and support counts, not standard errors. A conversion or utilisation framing for Figure 16, since reachable language is an access boundary rather than a funnel denominator.

**Not carried:** Figure 15 does not show what anyone watched. Figure 16 does not establish a language-selectivity effect or imply that viewers should consume languages in proportion to supply. Figure 17A does not define a segment, and 17B's Baseline slope is not an independent behavioural result. Figure 18 does not establish causality in either direction, and its quadrants are not materiality thresholds. Figure 19 does not establish a programme effect, and the supported-versus-all difference also carries population selection. Across all of them, Baseline against Final is descriptive, not within-profile persistence.

**Source data:** Figures 15, 16 and 19 are drawn from the published tables listed in the figure index — [`catalogue_multilinguality_by_program_type.csv`](../evidence/mart_audit/catalogue_multilinguality_by_program_type.csv), [`consumed_vs_opportunity_language.csv`](../evidence/viewer_diagnosis/consumed_vs_opportunity_language.csv) and [`test6c4b_programme_multilingual_sensitivity.csv`](../evidence/viewer_diagnosis/test6c4b_programme_multilingual_sensitivity.csv). Figures 17A and 17B come from the `ANCHOR_SHARE_BAND` rows of [`cross_origin_anchor_and_opportunity.csv`](../evidence/viewer_diagnosis/cross_origin_anchor_and_opportunity.csv). Figure 18 and its companion take their all-profile reference point from the `POPULATION` rows of that same table, and their six group bubbles from [`cross_origin_direction_cross_tab.csv`](../evidence/viewer_diagnosis/cross_origin_direction_cross_tab.csv), which publishes all nine behaviour × opportunity direction cells including the three that are empty. No profile-level data is used anywhere in this set.

## Test 5 — realistic opportunity conditioning — 2026-10

**Question:** once realistic opportunity is introduced, which Test 5 findings need visual explanation — the scale of the broad/regional difference in choice set, whether concentration moves consistently with that access difference, and whether the strongest Test 4 mechanisms survive being read inside opportunity families?

**Established:** broad and regional profiles differ far more in reachable catalogue than in meaningful breadth; concentration differences do not take one consistent sign across activity context; focused-successful and selective-core keep opposite completion signatures after opportunity conditioning; and selective-core replay stays clearly above peer context under broad access.

**Not established:** causal access effects, catalogue conversion or utilisation, recommendation exposure, statistical equivalence between broad and regional concentration, final segments, durable identities or headroom.

Figure 13 is zero-centred because the analytical question is the direction and size of the broad/regional difference once activity is held comparable. **Rejected:** splitting Figure 12 into 12A and 12B, because the central comparison was then spread across incompatible axes and had to be reassembled mentally. A headline figure for the genre incremental-model result, because it is supporting context and the gain is modest.

**Not carried:** Figure 12 does not imply that regional profiles use the catalogue more efficiently, and it is not a conversion or utilisation chart. Figure 13 does not prove a null effect or statistical equivalence; it shows that opportunity family imposes no single concentration direction. Figure 14A does not establish that broad access causes stronger completion, and the position of broad points relative to regional ones is not promoted into a separate finding. Figure 14B does not treat the large Baseline regional selective-core deviation as durable. Baseline against Final remains descriptive here, not within-profile change.

The `measured n` labels are profiles meeting each metric's evidence threshold. No profile-level data is added to the repository, and no separate figure-data table was needed.

## Test 4 — post-choice response and concentration mechanism — 2026-09

**Question:** what post-choice behaviours sit underneath concentrated viewing, and which of those findings are important enough to communicate visually before opportunity is added in Test 5?

**Established:** post-choice response is not one generic stickiness construct; validated resume is an intermediate pathway; replay requires a fixed observability horizon; and the same high concentration can correspond to materially different completion and replay behaviour as meaningful breadth increases. Several of those directions survive separate watch-hours and active-days controls.

**Not established:** final viewer segments, viewing headroom, causality, recommendation failure, durable viewer identity, or that replay necessarily occurs on the dominant title. Entitlement, catalogue reachability, language opportunity and other pre-choice constraints remain to be tested.
