# Visual decision log

## Test 7 — longitudinal behaviour of the same viewers — 2026-10

**Question:** Test 6D had established that cross-origin behaviour carries information inside the candidate families, but every family reading so far described one window. Test 7 followed the same 8,199 eligible viewers across both windows and found that families recur more often than exact states, that consumption can change sharply under a stable label, that post-choice outcomes diverge, that outside-origin viewing recurs in forms that are not all discovery, and that each family resolves into different pathways. Those findings are about movement, overlap and heterogeneity — exactly what prose and single averages flatten — so the series had to make each relationship visible without implying a universal rule.

**Established:** classification movement between families is large and uneven; family recurrence (4,883) substantially overstates exact-state recurrence (2,583), most visibly inside Residual; staying and leaving correspond to different consumption histories depending on the family; completion, continuation and replay change differently on the same supported viewers; outside-anchor recurrence is common but is often recurrence of the same outside title; families differ in anchor structure, actual outside allocation and within-family peer-relative spread; and joint breadth-and-hours expansion occurs along different routes in every family.

**Not established:** final segments, causal mechanisms, an independently calibrated propensity, the capacity of any viewer to watch more in future, or incremental headroom. Joint expansion is historically realised, not a qualification rule, and the provisional candidate pool is not shown.

### How to read each figure

**Figure 21 — where paired viewers moved between candidate families.** Rows are the six Baseline candidate families and columns the six Final families, for the 8,199 profiles eligible in both windows. Each cell prints the profile count above and the percentage of its *own Baseline row* below, so each row has its own denominator, shown beside it (Focused Successful n = 1,295, Residual n = 4,108); paired Final family totals sit under the columns. Blue shading encodes that row share, so colour compares routes within a row, not counts across the grid. All 36 routes appear, zero flows included; a dagger marks cells of 1–29 profiles, which are thin descriptive cells. Compare the diagonal across rows: Broad Distributed keeps 73.3% and Residual 71.8%, while Selective Core keeps 11.1% and sends 65.9% to Residual. This is classification movement between relatively ranked states — a move into Residual is not failure, and staying Broad Distributed is not saturation.

**Figure 22 — family recurrence versus exact-state recurrence.** Each bar divides a population into three mutually exclusive statuses: blue for the same exact concentration × breadth state, orange for the same family but a different state, grey for a different family. The top bar is all 8,199 paired viewers; the others are each Baseline family, with the bar's denominator printed at the right and each segment labelled with count and share of that bar. Read the blue and orange together as family recurrence (2,583 + 2,300 = 4,883) and blue alone as exact-state recurrence. Family construction drives the gap: Broad Distributed is the single state C1_B5, so it can have no orange segment, and the single-state families Selective Core, Access-Sensitive Neighbour and Successful First-Pass likewise; Residual spans 15 observed states, so 2,019 of its 2,949 stayers moved internally. A stable family label is not evidence of stable behaviour.

**Figure 23 — whether staying or leaving meant more viewing depended on the family.** The twelve rows are the stayed and left groups of each Baseline family; together they partition the 8,199 paired viewers by Baseline family. The left-hand table gives each group's `n` and its Baseline median meaningful titles and qualified hours — medians of that subgroup, not of the whole family. Panel A shows change in meaningful titles, panel B change in qualified watch hours. In both, teal is stayed and purple is left; the dot is the median of each viewer's own Final-minus-Baseline change, the bold number beside it repeats that median, and the shaded bar spans the 25th to 75th percentiles of individual changes; the vertical line is zero change. Compare the two rows within a family: Broad Distributed stayers added a median 14 titles and 5.5 hours without leaving C1_B5, while Focused Successful stayers sat at zero and its leavers gained 3 titles and 2.6 hours. A median change is not the difference between the two window medians, and the shaded bars are observed interquartile ranges, not confidence intervals. These are direct individual changes; the Test 7.3 activity-conditioned residuals failed comparability review and are not shown.

**Figure 24 — post-choice responses diverged across family trajectories.** Three panels share the same rows: A completion, B episodic continuation, C 14-day replay. Each family appears as stayed, left and entered. The horizontal axis is each viewer's Final-minus-Baseline rate change in percentage points, with a dashed zero line. The grey line is the 25th–75th percentile range of individual changes, the dark circle the median and the orange diamond the mean. `dual n` is the number of viewers meeting that metric's support rule in **both** windows — at least five known outcomes for completion, at least five observed next-episode opportunities for continuation, at least three known outcomes for replay — so each metric has its own population; a dagger marks dual n under 30 (only Access-Sensitive Neighbour stayers' continuation, 18). Read across the three panels within a row and across routes within a family: Focused Successful entrants gained a mean +9.46 points of completion while leavers lost 6.42. The outcomes do not form one engagement score; continuation depends on programme mix and next-episode opportunity, and replay on follow-up observability late in the Final window. Rows overlap across families and must not be summed.

**Figure 25 — cross-origin recurrence was not the same history for every family.** Rows are stayed, left and entered routes by family (Baseline family for stayed and left, Final family for entered), so they overlap across families. Panel A divides each route into three mutually exclusive parts: dark navy for viewers who watched the same outside-origin parent title in both windows, teal for viewers outside the anchor in both windows without repeating an outside title, and pale grey for viewers not outside in both windows; the dark share is printed. Repeating a parent title is not necessarily replaying an episode, and returning to a familiar outside title is not discovering a new one. Panel B shows individual change in outside-origin qualified hours: the dot is the median (orange, or rust where the median is negative) and the grey line the observed 25th–75th percentile range, with zero marked; a route can recur strongly while its median outside hours fall, as Broad Distributed leavers do. Panel C shows mean historically reachable outside-origin parent titles in Baseline (navy) and Final (orange), on an axis that starts near 350, not zero. Reachable means accessible under historical entitlement, not exposed, recommended or wanted. The three panels share populations but not individual-level intersections, and no causal chain runs between them.

**Figure 25B — how much viewing stayed inside each family's Baseline anchor.** This recaps the Test 6C–6D cross-origin evidence for the same 8,199 paired viewers, grouped by **Final** candidate family; it is context for Test 7.6, not a new test. The anchor is the original-language ecosystem holding the largest share of each viewer's qualified Baseline minutes, held fixed in Final — it is not the viewer's native language. Panel A: for each family, B and F bars split qualified minutes between the fixed anchor origin (navy) and outside it (teal), as family means of each viewer's allocation; `n` is the paired Final family. Panel B: the teal bar is the middle 50% of individual Final outside-viewing shares with the median marked, and the hollow orange diamond is the mean share of reachable parent-title-days outside the anchor; the two use different denominators (qualified minutes against reachable title-days), so their gap is neither a conversion rate nor a utilisation target. Panel C: Final outside viewing minus a candidate-blind peer expectation, in percentage points, using the **active-days** control; the line is the interquartile range of individual residuals, the dot the supported-group mean, the dashed line zero, and `n` the supported peer-comparison population. Peers match anchor origin, anchor strength band, outside opportunity band and activity quintile — not exact state or family — so these residuals are population-relative, not within-state propensity. The watch-hours control is published separately. Because the anchor is the selected Baseline maximum, raw rises in outside share are subject to reference selection and regression to the mean.

**Figure 26 — six candidate families, different routes through the same history.** Each family row has three cells: stayed in family, left family and entered family. Each cell prints the number of viewers who increased **both** meaningful titles and qualified hours over that route's cohort size (for example 445 / 603), and the bar and percentage show that proportion within the route. The right-hand column states the finding the family label did not explain. Read across a row: Focused Successful leavers expanded at 74% while stayers did at 17%; Selective Core stayers expanded without leaving C4_B3; Access-Sensitive Neighbour's destinations diverged (100 of 172 to Residual expanded, none of 127 to Focused Successful); Broad Distributed stayers kept expanding. Some zeros follow from destination definitions — entering the narrowest Focused Successful states almost precludes broadening — rather than an absence of potential. Proportions are within each route, routes overlap across families, and joint growth is historically realised expansion, not an advancement threshold or prospective headroom. The 3,565 provisional candidates cannot be reconstructed by summing these cells.

**Reasoning:** the transition matrix is the natural form for flow among a fixed set of discrete classes, and row normalisation keeps each family's denominator honest. Part-to-whole bars make recurrence statuses additive within a population, which is the point of Figure 22. Figures 23 and 24 show distributions of individual change against zero because the question is direction and spread, not a single average. Figure 25 keeps recurrence, consumption and opportunity in separate panels because they answer different questions with different units. Figure 25B combines allocation, distribution and peer comparison because the Test 6 argument depends on all three. Figure 26 places joint growth inside each family's routes because the integrated finding is that pathways, not families, carry the interpretation.

**Rejected:** one overall post-choice mean chart as the principal family reading, which hid divergent routes. Family means without state or pathway heterogeneity, which would have treated labels as homogeneous trajectories. A ratio of qualified viewing to reachable catalogue presented as conversion. Treating recurring outside titles as continuing discovery. A breadth × hours relational-validation matrix as a headline figure in place of the longitudinal sequence — those relationships stay as secondary context in the methodology. An earlier Figure 23 overview of the whole population and the first, unrevised version of the stayed/left figure, both superseded by the published Figure 23. Any visual that presented the provisional candidate pool as final segmentation.

**Not carried:** none of the figures shows causality, future capacity or headroom. Figure 21 does not grade transitions as good or bad. Figure 22 does not imply that exact-state stayers consumed the same amount. Figure 23 is not activity-adjusted. Figure 24 does not combine outcomes. Figure 25 does not show individual-level links between its panels. Figure 25B does not adjust for exact state or family and is not a fresh Test 7 result. Figure 26 does not identify advancement candidates.

## Test 6D — cross-origin non-redundancy inside candidate families — 2026-10

**Question:** once the Test 4 Final candidate families are held fixed and cross-origin viewing is benchmarked against candidate-blind peers with comparable anchor, opportunity and activity, is there material variation left *inside* each family — and what does that variation look like in actual viewing?

**Established:** material within-family residual dispersion under both activity controls, with near-identical ordering across them; its magnitude differs by family. Inside Focused Successful, the diagnostic low and high tails differ by about 81 points in actual cross-origin viewing while their group-mean outside-anchor opportunity and Baseline anchor strength are nearly equal. Together with the rest of Test 6D this supports provisional carry-forward of cross-origin catalogue propensity as a non-redundant, cross-cutting observed dimension.

**Not established:** causal effects of access, programme mix or exposure; independent replication; persistence over time; high/low propensity segments; psychological traits or native language; additional watch time; realised or realisable headroom.

### How to read each figure

**Figure 20A — within-candidate residual dispersion.** Each row is one fixed Test 4 family, ordered by concentration quintile from C5 to C1, with its state codes beneath the name. The x-axis is the cross-origin residual in percentage points; zero is the inclusive candidate-blind peer expectation. Within a row, the thick bar is the middle half of profile residuals, the thin line runs from p10 to p90 and the marker is the median. Filled circles and solid lines are the watch-hours control; open squares and dashed lines the active-days control, offset vertically only so they do not overlap. Read the width of each row rather than its centre: a near-zero median can sit inside a 46-point middle half. The table gives both IQRs, the cross-control Spearman correlation and the common-supported n, which sum to 5,510. Spread narrows steadily from the most concentrated families to the least; part of that may be how few titles set a concentrated profile's share, and no null benchmark for it is published yet.

**Figure 20B — Focused Successful tail context.** The top panel compares the low residual tail (orange, below peers, n = 195) with the high residual tail (blue, above peers, n = 202) on one 0–100% scale. Read the two context rows first — outside-anchor opportunity and Baseline anchor strength — because they are the evidence: they differ by 0.2 and 1.0 points. The outcome row differs by about 81 points, but the tails were selected on the residual, so a large gap there is expected by construction and only translates the residual into actual viewing. The lower panel applies the same tail definition to every family and plots high-tail minus low-tail mean opportunity: Focused Successful is the best-balanced, the high tail had less opportunity in the Access-Sensitive Neighbour and Selective Core, and about 11 points more in Successful First-Pass. All values are group means, not matched pairs; the 92.75% high-tail mean is labelled 92.7% to match the journal text.

**Reasoning:** Figure 20A is an interval plot because the Test 6D claim is about spread inside fixed groups, and family means alone would hide it — a near-zero average is consistent with large positive and negative residuals in the same family. Both activity controls are drawn, following the deviation-chart convention, so a reader can see their agreement rather than take a correlation on trust; the table carries the numbers. Rows are ordered by concentration because that ordering exposes how spread narrows as concentration falls, which a reader should see rather than discover. Figure 20B puts context and outcome on one percentage scale so equal-looking context bars and a very unequal outcome bar can be judged together. The lower panel exists because Focused Successful is the cleanest case; showing it alone would have read as a selected example.

**Rejected:** a candidate-family mean residual bar chart as the headline, because it hides the heterogeneity Test 6D was designed to detect. Violin or box-and-whisker plots, because the published evidence holds percentiles rather than profile-level residuals or observed extrema, and inventing shapes would overstate what is known. A composite propensity score or viewed-over-reachable ratio, which is neither defined nor earned and invites a conversion-rate reading. A 100%-stacked display of high and low "segments", since no segmentation was performed and diagnostic quartiles are not behavioural boundaries. Showing only Focused Successful in 20A, which would hide that the Access-Sensitive Neighbour is wider and Broad Distributed far narrower. Leading 20B with the 81-point gap, because that gap is guaranteed by how the tails were chosen.

**Not carried:** neither figure restages the raw cross-origin means or the opportunity-band standardisation; the published tables cover them. The two controls share the same Final outcome and most peer-key variables, so their agreement is robustness to the activity specification, not replication or persistence. High and low tails are positional diagnostics selected on the outcome residual and cannot independently demonstrate a predictive trait. Neither figure quantifies additional catalogue engagement or incremental viewing — demonstrated cross-origin viewing may substitute for existing hours — and group-level context comparisons do not prove identical opportunity for individual profiles.

## Test 6 — language supply, consumed language and cross-origin movement — 2026-10

**Question:** which parts of the Test 6 argument are hard to carry in prose — the structure of multilingual supply, the distance between consumed language and reachable language, the shape of the anchor distribution, the comparison between behavioural and opportunity movement, and the size of the programme effect that remained after matching?

**Established:** multilingual supply is heavily structured by programme type; consumed-language concentration sits far above opportunity concentration in every access regime; Baseline anchor strength is continuous apart from a pile-up at single-origin viewing; cross-origin viewing moved several times further than the opportunity behind it, and opportunity direction barely predicts behavioural direction; programme type shifts cross-origin behaviour only modestly once opportunity and activity are comparable.

**Not established:** causal language or programme effects, language-openness psychology, a calibrated selectivity measure, segment membership, persistence or headroom. Baseline against Final remains descriptive, not within-profile longitudinal proof.

### How to read each figure

**Figure 15 — multilingual supply by programme type.** Each horizontal bar is one programme type and totals 100% of that type's parent titles; the x-axis is the share of titles, not of viewing. Grey is monolingual supply, and progressively darker blues are titles offering 2, 3, 4, 5 and 6 audio languages. **Figure 17A — Baseline anchor strength distribution.** The x-axis is Baseline anchor share in contiguous 5-point bands and the y-axis is the percentage of the 8,199 paired profiles in each band; bars touch because the bands are intervals, so read the shape as a distribution rather than as ranked categories. The top band is the exception, and the annotation says what it contains: near single-origin viewing by the lightest viewers with the least cross-origin reach. **Figure 17B — cross-origin change by anchor band.** Both panels share the same x-axis of Baseline anchor-share bands. The upper panel plots levels — grey for Baseline, blue for Final — against a dashed line showing 1 − anchor share; Baseline tracks that line because the bands are defined from it, so the Baseline slope is definitional rather than behavioural. The lower panel carries the informative comparison: Final minus Baseline in percentage points, labelled per bar, with profile counts printed underneath as support. Compare the change across bands, not the Baseline slope. Bands below 30 profiles are omitted, and the footer states the regression-to-the-mean caution: bands defined on Baseline values will drift toward the middle in Final without any behaviour change.

**Figure 18 — behaviour against opportunity change.** The horizontal axis is the average change in cross-origin opportunity share (title-days) and the vertical axis the average change in cross-origin viewing share (minutes), both in percentage points on one shared scale; bubble area is the number of profiles and the black cross is all 8,199 paired profiles. The dashed diagonal is a 1:1 mechanical response — viewing moving exactly as much as opportunity — so the distance from that diagonal is the point of the figure: opportunity shares moved a few points while viewing shares moved 14 to 25. Groups are defined by the sign of their viewing change, so their vertical position is signed by construction; read distance from the diagonal and bubble size, not quadrant membership. The two bars being near-identical (64% against 66%) is the finding. Panel B puts magnitudes on one percentage-point scale: for each group the hollow square is the mean change in opportunity share and the filled circle the mean change in viewing share, so the length of the connector is the discrepancy. Group means in panel B are signed by construction — compare magnitudes, not signs.

Figure 16 is a dumbbell because the quantity of interest is the *distance* between two measures computed on the same profiles; two separate bar charts would have made the reader subtract by eye. Figure 17 is deliberately split: 17A answers "is there a natural cutoff?" and 17B answers "where did movement actually happen?", and merging them would have hidden the definitional Baseline slope inside a distribution. Figure 18 uses one shared scale with a 1:1 diagonal because the entire argument is that behaviour moved further than opportunity; unequal axes would have destroyed that comparison. 18B exists because the quadrant chart answers the magnitude question well and the direction question poorly, and the direction result — opportunity direction barely predicting behaviour direction — deserved its own readable panel. Figure 19 plots on the outcome scale rather than on a deviation axis so that a ±2-point deviation is visibly small against a 40–50% base.

**Rejected:** a single chart merging anchor distribution and cross-origin movement, because one is a distribution and the other a change across bands. A deviation-only bar chart for Figure 19, because it would have made modest differences look dramatic by hiding the base. Dropping the thin groups from Figures 15 and 19 to tidy the picture — they are faded and labelled instead. Any uncertainty band on these figures: the published evidence carries group means and support counts, not standard errors. A conversion or utilisation framing for Figure 16, since reachable language is an access boundary rather than a funnel denominator.

**Not carried:** Figure 15 does not show what anyone watched. Figure 16 does not establish a language-selectivity effect or imply that viewers should consume languages in proportion to supply. Figure 17A does not define a segment, and 17B's Baseline slope is not an independent behavioural result. Figure 18 does not establish causality in either direction, and its quadrants are not materiality thresholds. Figure 19 does not establish a programme effect, and the supported-versus-all difference also carries population selection. Across all of them, Baseline against Final is descriptive, not within-profile persistence.

## Test 5 — realistic opportunity conditioning — 2026-10

**Question:** once realistic opportunity is introduced, which Test 5 findings need visual explanation — the scale of the broad/regional difference in choice set, whether concentration moves consistently with that access difference, and whether the strongest Test 4 mechanisms survive being read inside opportunity families?

**Established:** broad and regional profiles differ far more in reachable catalogue than in meaningful breadth; concentration differences do not take one consistent sign across activity context; focused-successful and selective-core keep opposite completion signatures after opportunity conditioning; and selective-core replay stays clearly above peer context under broad access.

**Not established:** causal access effects, catalogue conversion or utilisation, recommendation exposure, statistical equivalence between broad and regional concentration, final segments, durable identities or headroom.

## Test 4 — post-choice response and concentration mechanism — 2026-09

**Question:** what post-choice behaviours sit underneath concentrated viewing, and which of those findings are important enough to communicate visually before opportunity is added in Test 5?

**Established:** post-choice response is not one generic stickiness construct; validated resume is an intermediate pathway; replay requires a fixed observability horizon; and the same high concentration can correspond to materially different completion and replay behaviour as meaningful breadth increases. Several of those directions survive separate watch-hours and active-days controls.

**Not established:** final viewer segments, viewing headroom, causality, recommendation failure, durable viewer identity, or that replay necessarily occurs on the dominant title. Entitlement, catalogue reachability, language opportunity and other pre-choice constraints remain to be tested.
